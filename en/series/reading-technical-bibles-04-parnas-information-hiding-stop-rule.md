# **Reading Technical Bibles 04: Parnas — Information Hiding as a Stop Rule**

**0. Prologue**  
A GO/NO-GO gauge is not trying to explain a part; it is trying to stop you at the right moment. On the shop floor, the hard failures often look quiet: a drawing still “looks the same,” yet the revision reason changes the datum scheme, the inspection method, or the tolerance policy. When that happens, comparability breaks first, long before anyone argues about architecture. This essay starts from that stop rule and then returns to software, because Khononov’s book made the same pressure legible: change arrives unevenly, and the real cost is how widely one change forces coordination. That question points backward to Parnas as a transferable method. I am not taking Parnas as a target to critique; I am using him as a reference I want to reuse.

**1. Encounter**  
I used to hear “information hiding” and picture secrecy: hide implementation details so others cannot rely on them. After Khononov, the phrase started to sound less like etiquette and more like a rule, and I wanted to know what it was a rule for. I did not know Parnas, so my first contact with the original argument came filtered through modern vocabulary and examples. That filter works until a simple doubt appears: why does this advice keep sounding like a criterion, not just a pattern. Following the thread reveals that the criterion is about isolating changing design decisions, not about hiding code, and that is why it transfers across domains.

**2. One Sentence**  
Decompose by design decisions that are likely to change. (Between terms are summarized in the Appendix.) Parnas states the criterion in plain terms: “We propose instead that one begins with a list of difficult design decisions or design decisions which are likely to change.” The implication is that modularity becomes a rehearsal rather than a slogan: list plausible changes first, then draw boundaries so each change touches as few modules as possible. In Between terms, this is exactly the act of protecting the Contract of a View. The hidden decision is treated as Basis, stored in Sidecar with Evidence, so that when it changes, the ripple effect is forced to stop at the Gate rather than leaking across the boundary. ([PDF mirror](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/edu/2ip30/references/criteria_for_modularization.pdf), [ACM DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/361598.361623))

**3. Principle**  
Information hiding is not secrecy of code; it is the local ownership of a decision that is expected to change. The evidence in Parnas’s argument is operational: a good decomposition reduces the number of places that must be edited when one decision shifts. That makes the interface less about exposing functionality and more about protecting others from becoming dependent on the decision itself. The implication is that stable meaning is a design output, produced by preventing decision drift from leaking across boundaries. That principle aligns with Between by treating comparability as something you must actively preserve, not something you inherit from a clean diagram. In Between terms, the hidden decision belongs in Sidecar as declared Basis with traceable Evidence, so drift becomes explicit rather than silent.

**4. Method**  
The method begins with a change rehearsal: write down decisions that are likely to change, then restate each as a concrete edit request. Next, draw boundaries so each edit request stays local, and so that what crosses the boundary is only what must remain stable. Start with the field-shaped cases: if an inspection method or datum scheme changes while the layout looks familiar, the boundary is correct only when the change is confined to a declared Basis and its traceable Evidence, not smeared across multiple Views. The same rehearsal applies to software: if a data representation changes (the classic case is how a “line” or “point” is stored and accessed), a good boundary keeps most callers unchanged; if the change forces edits in many places, the “secret” has leaked. The implication is that decomposition becomes a loop with a measurable outcome, not a one-time architecture declaration. The bridge to Between is operational: a rehearsal that shows leakage triggers Gate; the minimum Evidence is whatever proves the Basis and Contract are still coherent before you proceed. If the rehearsal reveals leakage, treat it as STOP: do not proceed to implementation. Redesign the boundary until the likely change stays local.



**5. Range**  
In CAD drawings, “likely to change” often shows up in the revision reason, but not always as geometry. Material substitution is a quiet example: the Views can remain identical while the Basis changes, because the decision “what material this part is” controls downstream process windows and what evidence is required. A switch such as SUS304→SUS316 (with geometry unchanged) can keep dimensions comparable in appearance while changing corrosion assumptions, procurement constraints, and the meaning of acceptance evidence (mill certificates, heat numbers, traceability). The implication is that comparability breaks when the artifact looks stable but its Basis drifts silently. This is exactly where Sidecar helps: keep the View and its Contract stable enough to compare, and place the material decision into Sidecar as declared Basis with traceable Evidence, so the drift becomes explicit rather than smeared across multiple Views. In forms and spreadsheets, the same pattern appears as rule tables, lookup sources, and approval thresholds that drift while the layout stays familiar.

**6. Bridge**  
Brooks makes conceptual integrity a survival condition, and Evans treats boundary crossing as a translation problem with real ownership. Parnas supplies a third piece: a criterion for drawing boundaries so that routine change does not force a system-wide renegotiation of meaning. The evidence is that the criterion predicts maintenance pain before it arrives, because it asks which decisions will churn and constrains their blast radius. The implication is that later vocabularies about coupling become useful not as competing theories, but as diagnostic lenses for enforcing the original criterion under pressure. Seen this way, Khononov’s “coupling” vocabulary is a modern restatement of Parnas’s containment criterion, packaged for daily design decisions. That is where Between can sit: Contract states what must remain comparable, Gate states where to stop when it is not, and gauge tests are the quick GO/NO-GO checks that detect leakage early enough to recover. In Between terms, the practical order is Gate→Evidence→Contract: decide where you will stop when drift appears, decide what proof is needed to continue, and only then minimize what must be promised at the boundary. A gauge test is the smallest repeatable check that detects cross-boundary leakage early enough to recover.

**Postscript**  
In the 3×3 lens, this episode thickened Contract×SSOT and Gate×Operation. SSOT means single source of truth. Parnas’s criterion works by fixing what the boundary promises and by turning change rehearsal into a repeatable stop condition in daily work. Contract×SSOT clarifies what the boundary promises as a reference surface, and Gate×Operation turns change rehearsal into a daily stop rule.

**7. Re-reading Kit**  
Read with one operational question: which decision is this boundary trying to make local, and how will leakage be detected. Each time a benefit is claimed, rewrite it as a change rehearsal with a named edit request and a predicted propagation path. Then write a one-line Sidecar note for that rehearsal: the Basis that must be held fixed for comparability, the Evidence that would prove it, and the Gate condition that triggers a stop when it is missing. If you cannot write a Gate condition, the boundary is probably decorative rather than protective. The bridge is that the same kit can be applied to code, drawings, and forms by changing only the meaning of View, not the method.

**Appendix: Sidecar template (portable)**  
View: the projection artifact used for action and comparison (drawing view, API surface, sheet output).  
Contract: what must remain stable and checkable at the boundary.  
Basis: the declared footing that makes comparisons meaningful (frames, units, identity rules, assumptions).  
Evidence: the minimal trace that lets others re-check the Basis later.  
Gate: the explicit stop point that triggers recovery when Basis or Evidence is missing.  
Change rehearsal: a short list of “likely to change” decisions and the boundaries they should touch if the decomposition is working.
